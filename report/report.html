1. Problem statement

Researchers look for earlier clinical trials similar to their own before starting a new one, but their search queries often leave out relevant medical terms. In our data, 59% of the trials that mention both metformin and type 2 diabetes also mention insulin, a word a searcher might not type. The aim of this project is to recommend those missing keywords: given the medical keywords in a query, predict which other keywords usually appear with them, so they can be added before searching. We implement the collaborative filtering model proposed by Xiao Zhou (Stanford CS229, 2020) and test it on real trial records.

2. Dataset details

SourceClinicalTrials.gov API, downloaded on 11 October 2026
Records usedThe first 50,000 trials returned by the API, out of 606,601 registered
Text used per trialBrief title, official title, brief summary, detailed description, conditions, eligibility criteria
Keyword vocabularyMeSH 2026 (Medical Subject Headings): 30,512 terms with 265,712 names and synonyms
Keywords kept1,237, the ones found in at least 0.5% of trials
Trials kept49,896 with at least two keywords (median 25 keywords per trial)
Split39,918 training, 4,989 validation, 4,989 test

Keywords are found by matching trial text against every MeSH name and synonym, so "breast cancer" and "Breast Carcinoma" both become Breast Neoplasms. Two cleaning steps were needed. Abbreviations are matched case-sensitively, so the "who" in "patients who" is not read as WHO. And 50 common words that MeSH uses in a different sense are ignored on their own: "will" was being read as Volition in 67% of trials.

3. Approach

Each trial becomes a column of TF-IDF scores, one row per keyword. The model predicts each keyword's score in a trial from the other keywords in the same trial:

R̂ = ReLU((W ⊙ Θ) R + b)

  • W is fixed and gives more weight to pairs of keywords that often appear together (cosine similarity). Θ and b are learned with mini-batch gradient descent on squared error with L2 regularisation.
  • To evaluate, half of each test trial's keywords are hidden. The model then recommends 3 new keywords, and we measure the share that were really in the trial (precision@3).
  • Hyperparameters (learning rate, τ, k, λ) are tuned on the validation set, following the paper.
  • Our additions: three baselines to compare against (popularity, item kNN, SVD), early stopping on the validation set, and ranking recommendations by how much the query raises them (lift), so the demo suggests specific keywords instead of common ones such as Patients.

The whole project runs on Google Colab with the notebook colab_run.ipynb. On Colab's free CPU, training the final models takes 38 minutes (Figure 1). The full hyperparameter search, 74 training runs, took 28 minutes on a laptop. A test file checks the gradient calculations.

5. Conclusions

  • The paper's model works. On the test set it finds hidden keywords 36 times as often as random choice (precision@3 of 0.434 against 0.012), close to the paper's own result (0.479 against 0.017).
  • The paper only compared against random choice. Recommending the most common keywords already gives 0.394, and a simple item kNN baseline, which needs no training, does better than the model (0.469).
  • Training longer makes the recommendations worse even while the training loss keeps falling (Figure 3). Stopping early on the validation set gives the best version of the model (0.446).
  • Precision rewards common keywords such as Patients. Ranking by lift gives more specific and more useful suggestions (Figure 2), at some cost in precision.

Reference: X. Zhou, "Collaborative Filtering on Keywords Recommendation for Clinical Trial Records", CS229 project report, Stanford University, 2020.
